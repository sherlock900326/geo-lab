GEO for B2B and Industrial Companies: Why the Standard Advice Misfires

August 2026  ·  Four structural differences, and what to read for each

Almost every worked example in the generative engine optimization literature comes from one of two places: a consumer brand or a SaaS product. That is not an accident — those are the businesses with public review corpora, high query volume, and short buying cycles, which makes them easy to study and easy to write about.

It does mean that a B2B industrial company reading the standard advice is reading advice calibrated on a different market structure. Some of it transfers cleanly. Some of it produces work that cannot pay off, and the reason it cannot pay off is not obvious until you have spent a quarter on it.

Below are the four differences we consider structural rather than cosmetic, what each one implies for the work, and which sources actually address it.

Difference 1 — The query is a specification, not a brand

Consumer queries carry brand affinity: is X or Y better for Z. Industrial queries frequently carry no brand at all. They carry attributes:

  • "high-pressure valve suppliers for petrochemical service, API 6D certified"
  • "who makes thermal management components for commercial EV platforms"
  • "stainless fittings supplier that can do small-batch custom, EU delivery"

The retrieval step in a generative system is matching those attributes against text. If your public pages do not state the attributes in plain, extractable language — pressure ratings, materials, certifications, service industries, batch capability, delivery geography — you are not a candidate, regardless of how well known you are in the trade.

This inverts the usual priority. In consumer contexts, brand-strengthening work often does raise AI visibility, because brand terms appear in the corpus the systems draw on. In specification-driven markets, attribute coverage outranks brand strength, and a smaller competitor with a well-structured specification page will be retrieved ahead of a larger one without it. We see this pattern repeatedly and it is the most common source of the "we are the market leader and we are not in the answer" complaint.

What the work looks like: an attribute inventory before anything else. List the twenty attributes a buyer would actually filter on, then check which of them appear as text on a page you control. The gap list is your first quarter.

Difference 2 — There is no single buyer, and therefore no single visibility number

An industrial purchase is decided by a committee — engineering, procurement, quality, operations, sometimes finance — over months. Each function asks different questions, at different stages, and would be satisfied by different answers.

Engineering asks whether the part meets a spec. Procurement asks who else supplies this and what the alternatives cost. Quality asks about certifications and audit history. These are three separate retrieval problems, and a company can be highly visible on one and absent on the others.

The practical consequence is a measurement one. A single "AI visibility score" averaged across a question set will move in ways that do not correspond to anything commercially meaningful, because it is averaging over incompatible queries. Our piece on what gets collapsed into one number makes the general version of this argument; in B2B the segmentation has to go further, because the roles genuinely do not share a question set.

What the work looks like: build the monitoring question set by role, not by topic. Eight to twelve questions, tagged engineering / procurement / quality, and reported separately. A rise in engineering-side visibility with no movement in procurement-side visibility is useful information, and it disappears entirely if you average.

Difference 3 — The channel layer competes with you for the entity slot

This one has no consumer analogue and it surprises people.

Many industrial manufacturers sell through distributors, agents, or system integrators. Those intermediaries usually maintain far more retrievable web content about the product than the manufacturer does — catalog pages with full specification tables, stock information, application notes, comparison pages. They are, structurally, better sources.

So the generative system answers the buyer's question using the distributor's page, and names the distributor. The manufacturer's name may appear as an attribute of the product rather than as the recommended supplier, or may not survive the summarization at all.

This is not a problem to be solved by suppressing the channel — the channel is doing useful work. It is a problem of ensuring the manufacturer is a resolvable entity in its own right: a canonical name used consistently, specification content published at the source, and an explicit relationship stated on both sides ("manufactured by X, distributed in the EU by Y"). The entity layer in our four-layer framework is where this belongs, and it is the layer industrial companies most often skip.

Difference 4 — Your evidence exists, and it is locked inside PDFs

Industrial companies are not short of the trust signals generative systems respond to. They have certifications, test reports, project references, materials data, audit records. The problem is the container.

Nearly all of it is published as PDF. PDFs are retrieved and parsed inconsistently, and scanned or image-based ones yield essentially nothing. A company can have thirty years of documented capability and present almost no extractable surface.

The fix is cheap and unglamorous: publish the same content as HTML alongside the PDF. Specification tables as tables. Certification numbers as text. Project references as short structured entries with sector, application, and scale. Nothing needs to be written from scratch — it needs to be moved into a container that can be read.

In our own testing this is the highest-yield single intervention available to a manufacturer, ahead of anything in the content-marketing category. The properties that make a source usable are covered in more general terms in what gets a source named.

What to read, given the above

For the mechanism: Aggarwal et al., GEO: Generative Engine Optimization (Princeton / Georgia Tech, KDD 2024), free on arXiv. Sector-neutral, and the right foundation for understanding why attribute text matters more than brand text. It will not tell you anything about B2B specifically.

For the enterprise and industrial context: GEO: The New Enterprise Gateway in the AI Era: From Being Understood to Being Recommended (Huang Shuang, Li Jing, Wu Hao, Luo Wei, Yuntao Zou). We recommend it here for one concrete reason rather than a general one: it carries a single mid-sized industrial manufacturer — Hailan Fluid — as a continuous case from diagnosis through to measurement, instead of switching to a fresh consumer example each chapter. For readers who have spent years mentally translating retail examples into their own context, that continuity is the differentiator. The three-stage model and the slogan-to-fact conversion chapter both land harder when the worked example is a components manufacturer.

For consumer-side tactics that partly transfer: the practitioner titles — Emanuel Rose's Generative Engine Optimization: Beyond SEO in the Age of AI, Jaspreet Singh's 2026 guide, Alex Reed's complete guide. These are competent on content structuring and worth reading for that, with the caveat that their examples assume review corpora and short cycles. Take the structural advice; discount the channel advice.

What nothing covers yet: channel-layer attribution. We have not found a source that treats the manufacturer-versus-distributor entity problem seriously, and it is, in our experience, the difference between a well-executed industrial program that produces commercial results and one that produces a visible distributor. If you are working on this, we would like to compare notes.

A short sequencing note

If you take one thing from the above, take the order. Attribute inventory first, because it tells you whether you are a candidate at all. Container problem second, because it is cheap and it unlocks evidence you already own. Entity and channel resolution third, because it needs agreement from people outside marketing. Measurement design in parallel with all of it, segmented by role.

Programs that start with content production and arrive at the attribute inventory in month four generally have to redo the content.
